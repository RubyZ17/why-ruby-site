03 / Proposed undergraduate thesis

When the Body
Refuses a Name.

Desire, Excess, and Becoming in Wilde’s Salomé and Woolf’s Orlando

I plan to compare Wilde’s one-act play, often discussed in relation to Symbolism and Decadence, with Woolf’s experimental, fantasy-inflected mock-biography. I am interested in how literary form shapes desire and gendered subjectivity, and how the two works explore the possibilities and limits of a changing self.

❦
01

Form & subjectivity

How do the two works’ distinct literary forms make desire and gendered subjectivity legible in different ways?

In Salomé, dialogue, repetition, gaze, and theatrical spectacle concentrate desire in embodied action. In Orlando, fantasy, biographical parody, and narrative play unsettle the idea of a coherent self.

02

Desire & world

How does desire shape the protagonists’ ways of perceiving and inhabiting their worlds?

Salomé’s desire for Jokanaan becomes entangled with looking, bodily fixation, possession, and violence. In Orlando, longing for love, life, and creative fulfillment unfolds across changing gendered and social conditions.

03

Historical time & modernism

How does Orlando use historical time to represent changing gender conventions, social conduct, and ideas of selfhood—and what emerges when it is read alongside Wilde’s fin-de-siècle drama?

Rather than classifying Salomé as a modernist work, I want to examine how its fin-de-siècle aesthetics resonate with, and differ from, questions Woolf explores through modernist form.

Visual references: Aubrey Beardsley, The Climax (1894), and a public-domain 1928 portrait of Virginia Woolf.

An emerging undergraduate thesis project, not a completed argument.

04 / Completed research · Hemingway

The Sovereign Sip:

Alcohol, Embodied Agency and Gender Performativity in Hemingway’s Early Fiction

Sole-authored article · Minor revisions in progress · The Hemingway Review

This article asks what alcohol does in a literary scene, rather than only what it signifies. I develop the concept of the “Sovereign Sip” to describe a bounded drinking episode in which alcohol materially alters bodily experience and temporarily redistributes agency within a social relation.

✦

Research question

How does alcohol participate in shaping bodily experience, gendered conduct, and social relations?

Theoretical approach

Through close reading, I brought new materialist approaches to matter into conversation with Judith Butler’s theory of gender performativity and Foucault’s concept of heterotopia, while drawing on scholarship on intoxication and embodied experience. These frameworks helped me examine alcohol as a material participant in social relations without treating it as an independent force that determines human action.

01

Jake Barnes

Jake’s intoxication exposes the fragility of masculine self-possession. Alcohol offers a temporary way to manage emotional vulnerability, but it cannot repair his bodily injury or resolve his relationship with Brett.

02

Brett Ashley

Brett’s public drinking unsettles male-coded social spaces and gender conventions, yet her agency remains vulnerable to renewed demands for conventional femininity.

03

Jig

Jig’s comparison of Anís del Toro to absinthe turns taste into a form of embodied judgment that exceeds her partner’s rationalizing language, without resolving the inequality of their relationship.

Central claim
Agency without emancipation

The “Sovereign Sip” names neither simple intoxication nor lasting freedom. Alcohol can open temporary possibilities for embodied agency, but those possibilities remain constrained by bodily limits and social power.

The concept traces a bounded event: how embodied agency emerges, what it makes possible, and where it reaches its limits.

Visual references: Raphaelle Peale, Still Life with Wine Glass (1818), and Pieter Claesz, Stoneware Jug, Wine Glass, Herring and Bread (1642).

05 / Why comparison?

Comparison should change the question—not simply place texts side by side.

My training in English and French has encouraged me to think across languages, genres, and intellectual traditions.

01
Across genres

What becomes possible in drama that a novel makes possible differently? How does form shape the problem it appears to represent?

02
Across traditions

What changes when a text is read alongside another literary or philosophical tradition, rather than within a single national canon?

03
Across methods

How can close reading and theory remain accountable to textual detail while opening onto broader questions about subjectivity and power?

03
A question I bring

Desire as a relational process

Across my projects, I am increasingly interested in desire as something that develops through a subject’s relations with others and the world—not simply as an expression of a fully formed self. Such relations may open new possibilities for agency while also reproducing existing constraints.

Can desire transform subjectivity without escaping the social relations and norms that shape it?

This question connects my work on Salomé, Orlando, and Hemingway, while remaining open to further development.
Where form enters

I see literary form as a way to investigate these questions, rather than an end in itself. Wilde’s theatrical spectacle and Woolf’s experimental mock-biography, for example, make desire and changing subjectivity legible in different ways. Comparative reading can then connect these formal choices to broader questions of gender, history, and modernity.

My aim is not to assume that our projects are identical, but to explore how my questions about desire and gender might enter into conversation with your work on decadence, modernism, and literary philosophy. See also: Publications · Talks

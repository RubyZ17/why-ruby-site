02 / Proposed undergraduate thesis

When the Body
Refuses a Name.

Desire, Excess, and Becoming in Wilde’s Salomé and Woolf’s Orlando

I plan to compare Wilde’s one-act play, often discussed in relation to Symbolism and Decadence, with Woolf’s experimental, fantasy-inflected mock-biography. I am interested in how desire shapes gendered subjectivity, how each protagonist understands the world through desire, and how changing historical conditions reshape the possibilities of becoming.

01

Desire & subjectivity

How does desire shape the protagonists’ understanding of themselves and others?

Salomé’s intense desire for Jokanaan raises questions about looking, possession, agency, and violence. In Orlando, the desire to love and to live unfolds as the protagonist’s gender and historical world change.

02

Gender & becoming

Can a subject become someone different without escaping the norms that shape them?

Rather than treating identity as a fixed essence, I examine how bodily change, social recognition, and relationships complicate the process of becoming a subject.

03

Form & historical time

What becomes visible when a play and a fantasy-inflected mock-biography are read together?

The contrast between Wilde’s theatrical intensity and Woolf’s shifting historical perspective helps me ask how different forms represent desire, gender conventions, and changing ideas of selfhood.

This is a proposed undergraduate thesis, not a completed argument. I do not treat Salomé as a modernist work; rather, I explore how its fin-de-siècle aesthetics resonate with and differ from Woolf’s modernist concerns.

03 / Completed research · Hemingway

The Sovereign Sip.

Alcohol, Embodied Agency, and Gender Performativity in Hemingway’s Early Fiction

Article under minor revision · The Hemingway Review

This article examines alcohol not only as a cultural symbol, but as a material participant in literary scenes. I develop the concept of the “Sovereign Sip” to describe a bounded drinking episode in which alcohol’s material effects temporarily reshape embodied agency within gendered social relations.

Research question

How does drinking change what a body can do—and what it cannot escape?

Theoretical approach

Through close reading, I brought materialist approaches to matter into conversation with Judith Butler’s theory of gender performativity and Foucault’s concept of heterotopia. I used these frameworks to consider how bodily experience, material objects, and social power interact without treating alcohol as a force that determines human action.

Textual focus 01

The Three-Day Blow

Drinking and masculine self-understanding: the temporary management of vulnerability and social expectations.

Textual focus 02

The Sun Also Rises

Public drinking, gender performance, and the limits of agency within intimate and social relations.

Textual focus 03

“Hills Like White Elephants”

Taste and embodied judgment as ways of perceiving a relationship beyond the language used to explain it.

Central concern

Agency without emancipation

The project considers how drinking may open temporary possibilities for embodied agency without resolving the bodily limits and social relations that constrain it.

This public overview is a concise description of the project, not the manuscript or a reproduction of its full argument.

05 / Where our questions meet

Desire, gender,
and modernist subjectivity.

My primary interests lie in modernism, gender, and desire. I am interested in desire not simply as an inner force that expresses an already formed self, but as a relation through which subjects come to understand themselves, others, and the world. I see several possible points of contact with your work.

Direct connection

Aestheticism, desire & gender

Your work on aestheticism, decadence, and gendered performance speaks to my proposed thesis on Wilde and Woolf. I am interested in how desire can create room for action while remaining entangled in the norms through which a subject is seen and recognized.

When does desire open new possibilities—and when does it reproduce the power relations it seeks to challenge?

Point of reference: Acting Aestheticism, Performing Decadence.

Long-term connection

Modernism & subjectivity

Your work on modernist idealism and your project on Eliot and d’Annunzio invite me to think about how literature reimagines experience, selfhood, and the relation between subject and world. My work on Woolf and Hemingway approaches related questions through desire, embodiment, and social conditions.

How do modernist texts represent a self that changes through desire and historical experience?

Point of reference: Modernist Idealism.

A question I bring

Desire as a relational process

Across my projects, I am increasingly interested in desire as something that develops through a subject’s relations with others and the world—not simply as an expression of a fully formed self.

Can desire transform subjectivity without escaping the social relations that shape it?

Where form enters

I see literary form as a way to investigate these questions, rather than an end in itself. Theatrical spectacle, experimental biography, and modernist narration make desire and changing subjectivity legible in different ways.
